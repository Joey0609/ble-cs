Try it: a frequency offset becomes range bias

Ideal model: Δd = −c·ε·Δt / 2, the same for phase and for time; the chart shows its size. A fast reflector (ε > 0) reads short. Read ε as whatever is left after calibration.

One formula covers both methods: the range bias is c times the frequency offset times the time between the two observations, over two. For phase-based ranging that time is the gap between the initiator's and the reflector's tones; for round-trip timing it is the reflector's turnaround. The chart shows the size of the bias; its sign follows epsilon: a reflector that runs fast makes both methods read short, a slow one long. Start at 12 ppm on the tone gap: about 12 cm. Switch to the turnaround: the same offset now costs about 32 cm. Then pull the offset down to 1 ppm: the bias falls to about 1 cm and 2.6 cm. That is the argument for calibration: the crystals cannot be made perfect, so the offset has to be measured and removed until what is left is well below 1 ppm. The model is ideal: no noise, no multipath, one offset for the whole step.
