Fundamentals of phase-based ranging: one tone

\( \theta_R = -2\pi\,\dfrac{d}{\lambda} + (\phi_I - \phi_R) \)

\( \theta_I = -2\pi\,\dfrac{d}{\lambda} + (\phi_R - \phi_I) \)

\( \Theta = \theta_I + \theta_R = -4\pi\,\dfrac{d}{\lambda} \pmod{2\pi}, \quad \lambda = \dfrac{c}{f} \)

\( d = -\dfrac{c}{4\pi}\,\dfrac{\Theta}{f} + k\,\dfrac{c}{2f}, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z} \) The oscillator phases cancel; one tone fixes d only up to a multiple of c/2f

  • A travelling wave: phase advances \( 2\pi \) per wavelength, \( \lambda = c/f \approx 12.3 \) cm
  • No start or end marker: the receiver sees phase, not arrival time
  • Precise, but the two-way phase repeats every \( \lambda/2 \approx 6.1 \) cm
  • One tone cannot tell 1.00 m from 1.06 m

One tone: fine but ambiguous → measure on many frequencies

Start with the wave itself. A tone at frequency f travels as a wave; one cycle spans one wavelength, lambda = c / f, about 12.3 cm at 2.44 GHz, and phase says where in the cycle the wave is when it arrives: it advances 2 pi for every wavelength of distance. The receiver compares that phase with its own oscillator. Now the two-way measurement. The initiator sends an unmodulated tone and the reflector measures its phase against its own oscillator; then the roles swap. Nothing is timed here: an unmodulated tone has no edge to timestamp, so each side only measures a phase. That phase is the phase accumulated over the distance, 2 pi d over lambda, plus the unknown phase difference between the two oscillators, with opposite signs. Adding the two measurements cancels the oscillator term and leaves the round-trip phase, 4 pi d over lambda. Solving for the distance with lambda = c / f gives d = minus c over 4 pi times Theta over f, plus any whole number of c over 2 f. Keep Theta over f in view: the next slide replaces it with the slope of Theta across frequency. The catch: phase is only known modulo 2 pi, so a single tone gives the distance only modulo half a wavelength, about 6 cm at 2.4 GHz. Below (↓): the same two-way exchange on many frequencies.
