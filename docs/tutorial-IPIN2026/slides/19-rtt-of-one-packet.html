Round-trip time (RTT) from a two-way exchange

\( A_I = \mathrm{ToA}_I - \mathrm{ToD}_I = \dfrac{2d}{c} + B_R \)

\( B_R = \mathrm{ToD}_R - \mathrm{ToA}_R \)

\( \mathrm{RTT} = A_I - B_R = \dfrac{2d}{c}, \quad d = \dfrac{c}{2}\,\mathrm{RTT} \) Each side measures only an interval on its own clock; the unknown clock offset and the turnaround cancel, the clock rate does not

  • ToD is time of departure; ToA is time of arrival
  • A known synchronization packet (CS_SYNC) lets the receiver locate its arrival
  • If the reflector's crystal runs \( \varepsilon \) fast, \( B_R \) comes out \( \varepsilon B_R \) long: RTT needs the mode-0 frequency offset too

Two intervals, two clocks: offset and turnaround cancel; mode 0 corrects the clock rate, leaving 2d/c

The same two-way trick works in time. The initiator sends a CS_SYNC packet and timestamps its departure; the reflector timestamps its arrival, waits, replies, and timestamps the reply's departure; the initiator timestamps the reply's arrival. Unlike a tone, a packet has a known bit pattern, so its arrival can be located. Each device only measures an interval on its own clock, A_I and B_R, so the unknown offset between the two clocks never enters. Subtracting the reflector's interval leaves the round trip, 2d over c. This mirrors the phase case, where the unknown oscillator phases cancelled. What cancels is the offset between the clocks, not their rates. The reflector counts its turnaround on its own crystal, and the carrier and the timer come from that one crystal. If it runs epsilon fast, B_R comes out epsilon times B_R too long: 12 ppm over a 176 microsecond turnaround is 2.1 ns, 32 cm. So round-trip timing needs the frequency offset from mode 0 just as phase-based ranging does; the timing-errors slide below has the numbers, and the calibration section shows how it is measured.
