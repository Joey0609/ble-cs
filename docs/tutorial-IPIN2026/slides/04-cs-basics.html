Basics of Channel Sounding: phase of one tone

\( \theta_R = -2\pi\,\dfrac{d}{\lambda} + (\phi_I - \phi_R) \)

\( \theta_I = -2\pi\,\dfrac{d}{\lambda} + (\phi_R - \phi_I) \)

\( \Theta = \theta_I + \theta_R = -4\pi\,\dfrac{d}{\lambda} \pmod{2\pi}, \quad \lambda = \dfrac{c}{f} \) Only phases are measured, never a time; the unknown oscillator phases cancel

  • A tone has no start or end marker: the receiver sees its phase, not its arrival time
  • The phase is precise, but it repeats every \( \lambda/2 = c/2f \approx 6.1 \) cm at 2.44 GHz
  • One tone cannot tell 1.00 m from 1.06 m

One tone: fine but ambiguous → measure on many frequencies

Start with a single frequency. The initiator sends an unmodulated tone and the reflector measures its phase against its own oscillator; then the roles swap. Nothing is timed here: an unmodulated tone has no edge to timestamp, so each side only measures a phase. That phase is the phase accumulated over the distance, 2 pi d over lambda, plus the unknown phase difference between the two oscillators, with opposite signs. Adding the two measurements cancels the oscillator term and leaves the round-trip phase, 4 pi d over lambda. The catch: phase is only known modulo 2 pi, so a single tone gives the distance only modulo half a wavelength, about 6 cm at 2.4 GHz. Next: the same two-way idea with time instead of phase.

Basics of Channel Sounding: round-trip time of one packet

\( A_I = \mathrm{ToA}_I - \mathrm{ToD}_I = \dfrac{2d}{c} + B_R \)

\( B_R = \mathrm{ToD}_R - \mathrm{ToA}_R \)

\( \mathrm{RTT} = A_I - B_R = \dfrac{2d}{c}, \quad d = \dfrac{c}{2}\,\mathrm{RTT} \) Each side measures only an interval on its own clock; the unknown clock offset and the turnaround cancel

  • A packet has a known bit pattern (CS_SYNC): the receiver timestamps its arrival at the correlation peak
  • No ambiguity: each metre adds 6.7 ns to the round trip
  • But coarse: 1 ns ↔ 15 cm, while the peak is ~870 ns wide

One packet: unambiguous but coarse → complements the precise but ambiguous phase

The same two-way trick works in time. The initiator sends a CS_SYNC packet and timestamps its departure; the reflector timestamps its arrival, waits, replies, and timestamps the reply's departure; the initiator timestamps the reply's arrival. Unlike a tone, a packet has a known bit pattern, so its arrival can be located by correlation. Each device only measures an interval on its own clock, A_I and B_R, so the unknown offset between the two clocks never enters. Subtracting the reflector's reported turnaround leaves the round trip, 2d over c. This mirrors the phase case, where the unknown oscillator phases cancelled. There is no wrap-around, so RTT is unambiguous. But the signal is only about 1 to 2 MHz wide, so the correlation peak is hundreds of nanoseconds wide, while 1 ns is 15 cm. With the LE 2M PHY, a 96-bit sequence and 20 dB SNR the theoretical single-packet bound is about half a metre, and practical results need averaging over many steps. Two corrections matter: the transmit and receive chain delays add up and must be calibrated (10 ns uncalibrated = 1.5 m), and the two clocks tick at slightly different rates, which Mode 0 measures (about 1 ppm is enough). Details: docs/Bluetooth_CS_Mode1_RTT_Estimation.pdf.

Basics of Channel Sounding: many tones

Repeat the two-way exchange on every CS channel \( f_k \): 72 channels, 1 MHz apart, 2402–2480 MHz. Example: d = 5 m.

\( d = -\dfrac{c}{4\pi}\,\dfrac{\partial\Theta}{\partial f} \)

Phase slope vs. frequency ∝ round-trip delay

\( d_{\text{amb}} = \dfrac{c}{2\,\Delta f} \approx 150\ \text{m} \)

1 MHz spacing: ambiguity grows from 6 cm to 150 m

\( \dfrac{c}{2B} \approx 2\ \text{m} \)

Bandwidth B ≈ 74 MHz sets how well two paths separate
The fix is to repeat the same two-way measurement on many frequencies. The round-trip phase is linear in frequency, so plotted against channel frequency it forms a straight line whose slope is 4 pi d over c. Left: what is measured, wrapped into 0 to 2 pi. Right: after unwrapping across channels. The missing points near 2426 MHz are CS channels 23 to 25, which are never used; that 4 MHz gap limits safe adjacent-channel unwrapping to about 19 m. This slope is the round-trip group delay: comparing phases at several frequencies recovers the delay that a single tone cannot show. With 1 MHz spacing the phase-vs-frequency pattern only repeats after 150 m. The total span of about 74 MHz sets the resolution for separating a direct path from reflections: about 2 m.

With multipath \( H(f_k) = \sum_\ell a_\ell\, e^{-j 4\pi f_k d_\ell / c} \): the shortest \( d_\ell \) is the true distance, so the slope alone is biased and the first path must be found.

In Bluetooth CS the reflector and the initiator each report a complex phase correction term, PCT, per channel and antenna path. Multiplying the two gives H[k], whose angle is the two-way phase. Multiplying the complex values avoids unwrapping each measurement on its own. Before estimating, remove the hardware loop phase with a calibration and make sure Mode 0 has corrected the frequency offset: both look exactly like extra distance. In a clean line-of-sight channel the slope fit is the best estimator. With reflections, H is a sum of paths and the slope is pulled towards them. An inverse FFT gives the channel impulse response, and the first significant peak is the direct path. Super-resolution methods such as MUSIC or ESPRIT separate paths closer than 2 m. Finally, the 150 m ambiguity is resolved with the RTT measurement from Mode 1 or 3. Full derivation: docs/Bluetooth_CS_Mode2_PBR_Estimation.pdf.
