Fundamentals of frequency calibration: the problem

What an offset breaks

Phase keeps turning between the two tones
\( \Delta d = -c\,\varepsilon\,\Delta t/2 \)

Intervals are timed on the reflector's own clock
\( \Delta d = -c\,\varepsilon\,T/2 \)

Tones land \( \varepsilon f_0 \) away from their channel

Sign: \( \varepsilon > 0 \) is a fast reflector, and both ranges read short

The problem: estimate ε in every subevent, to well below 1 ppm, and remove it before any phase or time is used. 1 ppm left over is 1 cm for phase (Δt = 69 µs) and 2.6 cm for time (T = 176 µs).
Core · Vol 6, Part A, §3.5 (frequency in Channel Sounding)

Both ranging methods assumed that the two radios share one frequency and one time base. They do not. Each radio derives its carrier from its own crystal, and every crystal is off by some parts per million: the initiator sits at f0 times one plus epsilon I, the reflector at f0 times one plus epsilon R. What matters is the relative offset, epsilon, the reflector's minus the initiator's, so a positive epsilon means the reflector runs fast. The specification allows up to 50 ppm, which at 2.44 GHz is 122 kHz. That offset breaks three things we relied on. Phase: the reflector measures the initiator's tone first, and the initiator measures the reflector's tone tens of microseconds later. In between, the two oscillators drift apart by 2 pi epsilon f delta t, which adds to the two-way phase and works against the propagation lag: a bias of minus c times epsilon times delta t over two, so with a fast reflector the range reads short. Time: the reflector times its turnaround on its own clock; a fast clock counts that interval long, too much is subtracted, and the range again reads short: the same form with the turnaround T, as on the round-trip slides. Tones: every tone lands epsilon f0 away from the channel the receiver is tuned to. So the calibration problem, stated formally: estimate epsilon in every subevent, well below 1 ppm, and remove it before any phase or time is used. The numbers: 1 ppm left over is 1 cm over a 69 microsecond tone gap and 2.6 cm over a 176 microsecond turnaround.
